What you must be able to do by the end of the course

The course file sets two learning outcomes. They, and they alone, determine whether you pass the unit.

Outcome 1 — The business plan. Starting from a business situation, develop a reasoned international e-commerce business plan, including:

  • the choice of target market;
  • consumer analysis;
  • competition analysis;
  • a web marketing strategy following the 4Ps (product, price, promotion, distribution and logistics), including payment gateways.

Outcome 2 — The requirements specification. Draw up the requirements specification for the e-commerce site, covering at least:

  • usability;
  • language adaptation;
  • visitor welcome;
  • customer retention;
  • the shopping cart;
  • the purchase process;
  • order tracking.

The level of mastery is assessed against four criteria: precision, coherence, integration of the concepts, and autonomy.

The common thread: a single project from start to finish

From the very first session, you choose a company (alone or in a team of 2 to 3) that wants to sell online in at least two foreign markets. Ideally, it is a real project: your own business project, your employer, a Brussels SME you know. Failing that, the teacher offers you a case.

Each module adds a building block to this project. The successive blocks become the chapters of your business plan and your requirements specification.

Milestone Content (2 to 4 pages) Deadline

J3 Web marketing strategy: 4Ps per market, acquisition channels, retention End of session 6
J4 Delivery, returns, customs and payments per market, with costs End of session 8

The 12 periods of independent work are devoted to this project: desk research, interviews with potential customers, drafting the milestones, financial calculations. Each milestone receives written feedback from the teacher.

Assessment

Final assessment (certifying)

The assessment covers the two learning outcomes:

  1. A written dossier submitted one week before session 12:
    • the international e-commerce business plan (20 to 30 pages excluding appendices);
    • the site’s requirements specification (10 to 15 pages, mock-ups in appendix).
  2. An oral presentation of 15 minutes followed by 15 minutes of questions, during session 12. In a team, each member presents and answers individually.

Pass threshold and level of mastery

To pass (50%), you must reach both learning outcomes: a business plan that contains all the required elements and a requirements specification that covers the seven sections, correctly and with sound reasoning.

Beyond the threshold, the grade takes the level of mastery into account:

Integration Use of the concepts from all five modules in a single, realistic project

Formative assessment

The in-session problem scenarios and milestones J1 to J5 are formative: they are not graded, but they receive feedback and prepare you for the assessment. Meeting the milestones is taken into account in the autonomy criterion.

You may use generative AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, DeepL…) to look for ideas, translate, rephrase or check a calculation, on three conditions:

How we work: problem-based learning

This course follows problem-based learning (PBL), the method born at the University of Maastricht and adapted here for adults returning to study1. The principle: you do not start with the lesson, you start with a realistic problem, drawn from the life of a company (most often BELGA CRAFT, the fictional company that runs through the syllabus). The team analyses the problem with what it already knows, identifies what it does not know, goes to find it out (in the chapter, in the sources, in the field), then comes back to produce an answer and confront it with the other teams. The teacher is a tutor: they ask questions, prompt, and only give “the” answer at the end, once it has been sought.

Each four-period session follows the same sequence, described in detail in the problem scenario of each chapter:

Phase Indicative duration What the team does What the tutor does
1. Analysis of the problem 30 min Clarifies the terms, states the problem in one sentence, lists what it thinks it knows (brainstorm), sorts these ideas into hypotheses, sets its learning objectives Observes, prompts with a question, does not correct
2. Research 45 to 60 min Reads the chapter, consults the sources and tools, sometimes goes into the field Points to the resources, not to the answers
3. Production 45 to 60 min Produces the required deliverable (note, grid, mock-up, calculation) Moves among the teams
4. Pooling 30 to 45 min Presents, confronts its starting hypotheses with what it found, listens to the other teams Summarises: what was seen, missed, and what remains open

Within each team, four roles rotate each session: the facilitator (gets everyone talking, keeps the thread), the scribe (notes hypotheses, objectives and decisions), the timekeeper, and the reporter (presents at the pooling). The syllabus chapters are the resource for phase 2: read them after the analysis of the problem, not before, otherwise the session loses its point. The “If you get stuck” section of each problem scenario gives leads, not answers.

The tools used in session are free or available in a free version (Google Trends, Similarweb free version, PageSpeed Insights, Eurostat, Looker Studio, Canva, Miro, the test environments of Mollie and Stripe). Situations that require data come with a file provided by the teacher.

  1. Problem-based learning is part of the andragogical approach, which brings together the methods specific to adult learning: autonomy, drawing on experience, the learner as an actor in their own training. A useful reference: Schmidt, H. G., “Problem-based learning: rationale and description”, Medical Education, 1983, for the Maastricht seven-step method.↩︎

How to read this syllabus

The course is organised into five modules, which follow the order of the teaching unit’s official programme, and into twelve chapters, one per session (module 5 has four, including the final assessment). The appendices gather the legal framework, ten case studies, a practice case, the templates to fill in, a toolbox, the glossary and the sources.

Each chapter follows the same structure:

  • an opening question, which you must be able to answer by the end of the chapter;
  • the session’s problem scenario: a concrete case, often drawn from BELGA CRAFT, which you analyse as a team before reading the lesson (see “How we work”); it replaces the classic exercises and sets out how the four periods unfold;
  • the lesson, in running text, with sourced and dated figures;
  • the BELGA CRAFT example, a fictional Brussels company that accompanies us up to the final assessment: in each chapter, you see what the expected deliverable looks like, applied to its case;
  • “Common mistake” and “For your project” boxes;
  • five self-check questions;
  • a video library and readings, with clickable links, the duration, and a line saying why to watch or read: the resources marked ★ are the minimum, the others complement them according to your time. Each video library ends with a “YouTuber version”: the same subjects, handled by creators you may already follow, to watch with the same critical mind as the rest.

Convention in this syllabus. Every figure comes with its source and its date. Figures age quickly: before using one in your business plan, check that there isn’t a more recent version. A figure without a source has no place in a business plan.

Module 1 · Module 1 · ~1 min read

The fundamentals of international e-commerce

Sessions 1 and 2 — 8 periods

This module corresponds to the first two points of the official programme: describe the technological context and the evolution of e-commerce, and distinguish the four main forms of e-commerce (B2C, C2C, B2B, B2G). Everything you learn here serves the “Project and business model” and “Target markets” sections of your business plan directly.

Self-assessment

Test yourself before moving on. Multiple-choice questions are marked with one click; for open questions, write your answer first, then compare it with the model answer.

Multiple-choice questions

  1. In light of chapters 1 and 2, why can it be said that factory-to-consumer platforms (Temu, Shein) illustrate both a market evolution (chapter 1) and a particular business model (chapter 2)?

    Temu and Shein define phase 5 (since 2024) described in chapter 1 and embody the factory-to-consumer model of chapter 2: no stock in Europe, very low prices, individual shipping of small parcels long exempt below 150 euros.

  2. Chapter 1 stresses that "every figure has a source and a date" and chapter 2 builds the Business Model Canvas of BELGA CRAFT. What common thread links these two requirements for a business plan?

    Both chapters push the same rigour: figures serve a line of reasoning (the context paragraph of BELGA CRAFT ends on a question that the business plan addresses), and the canvas forces you to link segments, value proposition, channels and costs to justified choices.

  3. The 1,400 Belgian SMEs present on Amazon generated 350 million euros in export sales in 2023, and 90% of the turnover of the 250 Brussels SMEs concerned came from abroad. What should be kept in mind when citing these figures?

    The course underlines that these figures come from Amazon (October 2024/2025, relayed by La DH): all the more reason to cite them with their source. They show that the marketplace has often been the gateway to exporting, without making it the only possible channel.

  4. An SME wonders whether it should integrate voice commerce and augmented reality from its very first international business plan. What does module 1 recommend?

    Chapter 1 is explicit: augmented reality and voice commerce exist but remain marginal. The real priorities are mobile (the primary screen of commerce) and AI, already "in the store" and "in front of the store".

Open questions

  1. Drawing on chapters 1 and 2, explain to a Brussels maker why the first concrete decision for selling abroad is not the choice of country but the choice of channel, and how market figures inform this choice.

    The first decision is the channel because it is the channel that structures everything else: margin, ownership of customer data, the foreign customer's trust, dependence and the initial investment (point 2.3). Three options, often combined: your own website (full margin, your own data, but an audience to build country by country), the marketplace (immediate audience and trust, but an 8 to 15% commission plus logistics, loss of data and strong dependence) and the combination of the two. The figures from chapter 1 inform this choice: the European market exceeds 900 billion euros and is growing by 5 to 7% per year, but in Belgium the growth is escaping local webshops (+3.4%) in favour of the platforms. For a small brand, this often argues for first testing a foreign market on a suitable marketplace (Etsy, Amazon.de) for six to twelve months, at low cost and with the trust carried by the platform, then opening a localised website when the volume justifies the investment and shifting loyal customers over to it to recover margin and data. The country is then chosen, based on the demand observed and the seven cross-border differences.

  2. Link the historical evolution of e-commerce (chapter 1) and the business models (chapter 2): how do the European rules adopted between 2021 and 2026 change the balance between the different models, in particular the factory-to-consumer model and the B2C of Belgian SMEs?

    Phases 4 and 5 show a regulatory tightening that rebalances the game. The VAT reform of 1 July 2021 (end of the exemption for small consignments, 10,000-euro threshold, OSS/IOSS one-stop shops) first framed intra-EU distance sales: a Belgian SME in B2C must apply the VAT of the customer's country above 10,000 euros, but the OSS one-stop shop simplifies the declaration. The Digital Services Act (2024) imposed new obligations on very large platforms, including Temu and Shein. Finally, the abolition of the customs exemption below 150 euros, replaced by a 3-euro duty per item on 1 July 2026, hits the factory-to-consumer model head on, since it relied precisely on millions of small, low-priced imported parcels: its cost advantage has shrunk. For the B2C of a Belgian SME, these rules reduce the price gap with the Chinese platforms and reward the strengths the SME can offer: a transparent total price, fast and reliable delivery, local payment methods, customer service in the customer's language. The history of the market and the choice of business model are therefore inseparable: the rules shift the competitive advantage from one model to another.

The context and evolution of e-commerce

Session 1 — 4 periods

Opening question

In 2025, Belgians spent 18.3 billion euros online, a record. Yet Belgian webshops grew only at the pace of inflation. Where did the growth go, and what does this mean for a Brussels SME that wants to get started?

Problem scenario 1: “Where did the growth go?”

The situation. Monday morning, 9 a.m., in the offices of hub.brussels. You are the team of junior consultants that the founder of BELGA CRAFT met during a drop-in session. She puts an article from La DH dated 5 June 2026 on the table: “Belgians spent 18.3 billion online in 2025, a record.” Then a second figure, highlighted in marker: “Belgian webshops, for their part, grew only by 3.4%.” And her question: “If the market is booming but not the Belgian shops, am I getting into something that’s dead on arrival? And why does everyone tell me ‘go on Amazon’ when my neighbour sells on bol, and my nieces now only buy on Temu?” She comes back on Thursday. She wants to understand, not a lecture.

What you must produce. A one-page note for the founder, with the five figures that matter to her (sourced and dated) and a reasoned answer to her two questions. Plus the comparison grid below, completed for Amazon.com.be, bol and Temu.

What does Temu gain by requiring an account before showing a search? And for your project. Before the end of the session: form your team (two or three people, or alone), choose your company and settle three questions: what does it sell? to whom? why go abroad? A real project is preferable; the teacher offers cases failing that.

1.1 A global market of 6,400 billion dollars

Retail e-commerce, that is, the online sale of goods to consumers, accounted for roughly 6,420 billion dollars worldwide in 2025, up 6.8% year on year, according to EMARKETER’s forecasts published in February 2025. The same institute expects 6,880 billion in 2026 and nearly 7,900 billion in 2028. Set against retail as a whole, e-commerce now weighs a little more than one euro in five (around 21% in 2026), and the share keeps growing by half a point a year.

Three regions concentrate about 80% of these sales: China, by far the world’s leading market, the United States and Western Europe. The fastest growth is found elsewhere: in Southeast Asia (Philippines, Thailand, Malaysia) and in Latin America, where a large middle class is buying online for the first time, straight from a smartphone.

Two orders of magnitude are enough for the rest of the course:

  • global e-commerce growth is around 7% per year, two to three times that of retail as a whole;
  • one retail purchase in five is now made online, with large differences between countries (more than one in four in China and the United Kingdom, fewer than one in ten in several southern European countries).

The global figures do have a limit, though: countries do not all measure the same thing. Some count goods only, others goods and services (travel, tickets, subscriptions). The orders of magnitude and the trends are reliable; comparisons down to the billion are not.

1.2 Europe and Belgium

In Europe, the annual report of Ecommerce Europe and EuroCommerce puts B2C e-commerce at 911 billion euros in 2025, up 7%, with a forecast of 961 billion (+5%) for 2026 (European E-commerce Report 2026, September 2026).

In Belgium, consumers spent 18.3 billion euros online in 2025, against 17.4 billion in 2024, a growth of 5.4%. More than nine Belgians in ten buy online. The top three sectors are fashion and footwear (2.7 billion), electronics (1.7 billion) and food and beauty (1.26 billion) (Becom, 2025 figures published in June 2026).

But the figure that matters for this course is a different one: the turnover of Belgian webshops grew only by 3.4%, roughly the pace of inflation. In other words, in volume, Belgian retailers did not sell more online in 2025 than in 2024. The market’s growth went elsewhere: to Amazon, which opened amazon.com.be in October 2022; to bol, the leader in Flanders and owned by the Dutch group Ahold Delhaize; to Zalando; and above all to the Chinese platforms. 62% of Belgians have already bought on Shein, Temu or a comparable platform, and the same proportion say they encountered a problem with their order there.

Two other figures from the same survey herald the rest of the course: 29% of Belgian consumers already use an artificial intelligence assistant to search for or compare products (they were 21% a few months earlier), and 36% say that social media sometimes influence their purchases.

The takeaway. For a Belgian SME, international e-commerce is not primarily a distant adventure. It is a matter of survival on its own market: foreign competitors are already in its home. Selling abroad also means learning to defend your place in Belgium with the same weapons as them: transparent total price, fast delivery, local payment methods, customer service in the customer’s language.

1.3 Thirty years of history in five phases

Phase 1 (1995-2005): emergence. Amazon and eBay were born in 1995, in the United States. Online payment is seen as risky, connections are slow, delivery is expensive. The first buyers are tech-savvy pioneers, the market is almost exclusively domestic. The bursting of the internet bubble in 2000-2001 carries off many pioneers (Boo.com, Webvan), but not the model.

Phase 2 (2005-2015): democratisation. Broadband, then the smartphone (iPhone in 2007), bring online shopping into everyday habits. Marketplaces take hold: Amazon opens its platform to third-party sellers, Alibaba becomes the Chinese giant, bol.com establishes itself in the Netherlands and then in Belgium. PayPal and the first payment providers reassure buyers. The first cross-border sales appear, often by accident: a German customer ordering from a Belgian site without the site having planned for it.

Phase 3 (2015-2020): the structuring of cross-border. Mobile becomes the majority of site traffic. Providers specialised in cross-border selling (duty calculation, currency conversion, logistics) appear, such as Global-e. Social media become a sales channel. Zalando, Vinted and Coolblue build pan-European brands. States begin to react: the General Data Protection Regulation (2018) and the regulation against unjustified geo-blocking (2018) are the first European texts designed for the online economy.

Phase 4 (2020-2024): acceleration and new rules. The lockdowns make online shopping surge, including among audiences that were not using it (food, seniors). On 1 July 2021, the European Union reforms e-commerce VAT: end of the exemption for small imported parcels, a single threshold of 10,000 euros for distance sales within the EU, creation of the OSS and IOSS one-stop shops. In 2024, the Digital Services Act imposes new obligations on platforms and marketplaces.

Phase 5 (since 2024): Chinese platforms and artificial intelligence. Temu, launched in Europe in 2023, and Shein see explosive growth thanks to very low prices, highly addictive apps and considerable advertising budgets. Both are designated “very large platforms” under the Digital Services Act in 2024. The Union reacts again: since 1 July 2026, the customs duty exemption for parcels under 150 euros has disappeared, replaced by a flat duty of 3 euros per item. In parallel, artificial intelligence assistants (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini) are changing the way people search for a product, and the first “agentic” purchases, where an assistant compares and orders for the consumer, are appearing.

Common mistake. Placing the VAT reform and the OSS one-stop shop “around 2015.” The reform dates from 1 July 2021 and it is one of the most important texts for your business plan: as soon as your distance sales to other EU countries exceed 10,000 euros per year, you apply the VAT of the customer’s country. We will come back to this in module 4.

1.4 Mobile and artificial intelligence

The smartphone has become the first screen of commerce. In most countries, the majority of visits to merchant sites are made on mobile, and a growing share of purchases. In Southeast Asia and Africa, many consumers have never bought any other way. The consequences are concrete: a site designed first for the computer, with long product pages, tedious payment forms and heavy images, loses part of its customers before they have even seen the price. Temu’s and Shein’s apps were designed for mobile from day one; that is one of the reasons for their success.

Artificial intelligence is already in the shop. It serves to translate and localise product pages (DeepL, tools built into the platforms), to write descriptions, to recommend products, to answer customers via chatbot, to detect payment fraud. For an SME, it is the first time that localising a site into three languages becomes affordable.

Artificial intelligence is also in front of the shop. When a consumer asks ChatGPT or Perplexity “which noise-cancelling headphones should I buy for under 300 euros?”, the assistant answers with a few products and a few sites. Being cited in that answer is becoming a stake comparable to ranking on Google fifteen years ago. We will come back to this in module 3.

Augmented reality (trying on glasses or placing a piece of furniture in your home via the phone’s camera) and voice commerce exist, but remain niches. Do not build a business plan on them.

The BELGA CRAFT example: situating your project in the landscape

BELGA CRAFT is a maker of handcrafted jewellery based in Brussels. She makes 450,000 euros of turnover, 85% of it through her online site, almost exclusively from Belgian customers. She employs three people and makes each piece to order, in three to five days. She wants to sell in Germany and the Netherlands within 18 months.

Here is how she uses this chapter’s figures in the first page of her business plan:

The European online sales market exceeds 900 billion euros and grows by 5 to 7% per year (Ecommerce Europe, 2026). In Belgium, online spending reaches 18.3 billion euros, but Belgian webshops no longer capture the growth (+3.4% in 2025, i.e. inflation), which is taken by foreign platforms (Becom, 2026). For BELGA CRAFT, where 85% of sales are made online to Belgian customers, staying on a single market amounts to depending on a market where foreign competition is intensifying. Germany and the Netherlands are the two largest neighbouring online markets; online jewellery there grows by 8% and 12% per year (sector figures, see appendix). Customers there already buy craft goods from abroad: the market exists, the question is one of model and means.

Note three things: each figure has a source and a date; the figures serve a line of reasoning (why international, why these countries) and are not lined up to impress; and the paragraph ends on the question that the rest of the business plan will address.

For your project. Write the equivalent paragraph for your company right now: three to five sourced figures, a line of reasoning, a question. You will improve it as the modules go by. It becomes the first page of your milestone J1.

Multiple-choice questions

  1. In 2025, Belgians spent 18.3 billion euros online (i.e. +5.4% year on year), but the turnover of Belgian webshops grew by only 3.4%, roughly the pace of inflation. How do you interpret this gap for a Brussels SME that wants to get started?

Open questions

  1. Explain why, for a Belgian SME, international e-commerce is not primarily "a distant adventure" but a matter of survival in its own market.

    Belgians' online spending is rising (18.3 billion euros in 2025, +5.4%), but Belgian webshops are stagnating (+3.4%, i.e. inflation): the growth is going to Amazon, bol, Zalando and the Chinese platforms (62% of Belgians have already bought from Shein, Temu or a comparable site). In other words, foreign competitors are already present in the Belgian market. An SME that does not react loses share at home. Learning to sell abroad means acquiring the same weapons as these competitors to defend your place in Belgium: a transparent total price, fast and reliable delivery, local payment methods (Bancontact), customer service in the customer's language. International expansion and defending the domestic market are therefore the same fight.

  2. Name two changes in European rules that occurred between 2021 and 2026 and explain in one sentence, for each, why it concerns an SME that sells online.

    First change: the reform of e-commerce VAT on 1 July 2021, with the single threshold of 10,000 euros for intra-EU distance sales and the OSS/IOSS one-stop shops. It concerns the SME because, as soon as its sales to other EU countries exceed 10,000 euros per year, it must apply and declare the VAT of the customer's country through the OSS one-stop shop. Second change: the abolition, on 1 July 2026, of the customs-duty exemption below 150 euros, replaced by a flat-rate duty of 3 euros per item. It concerns the SME because any item imported from outside the EU now bears this duty, which changes the total price displayed and reduces the price advantage of factory-to-consumer platforms. You could also cite the Digital Services Act (2024), which imposes new obligations on platforms and marketplaces.

  3. What is the order of magnitude of global retail e-commerce in 2025 and its annual growth, and why must these global figures be handled with caution?

    Global retail e-commerce represented roughly 6,420 billion dollars in 2025, up about 6.8% year on year (EMARKETER forecasts, February 2025); the order of magnitude to remember is about 7% annual growth, i.e. two to three times that of retail as a whole, and roughly one retail purchase in five is made online. These figures must be handled with caution because not all countries measure the same thing: some count goods only, others count goods and services (travel, tickets, subscriptions). The orders of magnitude and the trends are reliable, but comparisons down to the billion are not. You must therefore always cite the source, the date and the scope.

2.1 Who sells to whom: the four basic forms

The teaching unit’s programme asks you to distinguish four forms of e-commerce. They are defined by the nature of the seller and that of the buyer.

Business to Consumer (B2C)

A company sells directly to the end consumer. It is the most visible form: Zalando, Coolblue, Decathlon, the online shop of a brand or a craftsperson. Volumes are large, average baskets rather small, and marketing addresses the general public. The B2C customer is protected by consumer law: 14-day right of withdrawal, two-year legal guarantee, pre-contractual information, price display rules.

Internationally, B2C concentrates all the difficulties that this course will address: language, currency, the payment methods expected in each country, last-mile delivery, returns, the VAT of the customer’s country. In return, a German or Dutch consumer does not ask for a sales appointment: if the site is convincing, they buy.

Business to Business (B2B)

Companies sell to other companies: a wholesaler to retailers, a parts manufacturer to industrial firms, a software publisher to SMEs. Online examples range from Alibaba.com (a global supplier platform) to Ankorstore (a European marketplace where independent shops buy from brands), by way of wholesalers’ sales sites.

Online B2B was long behind B2C, but it exceeds it in value: orders are larger, often recurring, and professional buyers now want the same comfort as in their private life (clear catalogue, ordering in a few clicks, tracking). Three specifics: prices are often negotiated or tiered and hidden from non-customers; payment is made at 30 or 60 days rather than on order; the invoice is a central document. In Belgium, since 1 January 2026, structured electronic invoicing (the Peppol network) has been mandatory between VAT-registered companies: a Belgian B2B site must be able to issue it.

Consumer to Consumer (C2C)

Private individuals sell to other private individuals through a platform: Vinted for clothing, 2ememain (Marktplaats in the Netherlands) for a bit of everything, eBay historically. The platform sells nothing itself: it makes money through commissions, “buyer protection” fees, promotion options and advertising.

C2C lives on trust between strangers. All its know-how consists of manufacturing it: ratings, payment held in escrow until receipt, built-in shipping labels, moderation. Since 2023, the European DAC7 directive has required platforms to transmit to the tax authorities the data of sellers who exceed 30 sales or 2,000 euros per year: C2C is no longer a grey area.

Business to Government (B2G)

Companies sell to public administrations: municipalities, Regions, the federal State, European institutions. Public purchases go through public procurement procedures; in Belgium, the e-Procurement platform publishes the notices and receives the bids. B2G is not a “webshop” in the usual sense: prices, criteria and deadlines are set by the contracting authority’s requirements specification, and social and environmental criteria carry more and more weight. For an SME, B2G is a stable source of revenue but requires real administrative capacity.

Common mistake. Believing that these four forms are mutually exclusive. Most companies combine two or three of them. A furniture manufacturer sells to private individuals on its site (B2C), to architects and hotels via a professional area (B2B) and responds to municipal tenders for schools (B2G). Your business plan must say which form is central and which are secondary.

2.2 Hybrid and emerging models

B2B2C: selling to the consumer through an intermediary. The brand remains the seller, but the transaction goes through a platform that brings the audience: a third-party seller on Amazon or bol, an app on the App Store, a creator on Etsy. The advantage is immediate: millions of visitors from day one, payment and often logistics taken care of. The price to pay is threefold: a commission (of the order of 8 to 15% depending on the category on Amazon, more if the platform handles logistics), dependence on rules you do not control, and the loss of the direct relationship with the customer, whose data the platform keeps.

Direct-to-consumer (D2C): the brand without an intermediary. Conversely, some brands refuse distributors and marketplaces, selling only on their own site and, sometimes, in their own stores. They keep the distributor’s margin and the knowledge of their customers. But they must pay for each visitor (advertising, social media) and provide after-sales service on their own in each country. The VanMoof case, developed in the appendix, shows what happens when the second part is neglected.

Consumer to Business (C2B): the individual as supplier. Freelancers sell their services to companies via Malt or Upwork; photographers sell their images to image banks; content creators sell their audience to brands. C2B is of interest to this course for one reason: it is where many e-merchants find their translators, their photographers and their copywriters internationally.

Social commerce: buying without leaving the social network. Instagram, TikTok Shop or Facebook Marketplace allow you to buy directly in the feed. In China, livestream commerce (live video selling) represents a major share of online sales; in Europe, it remains marginal but is growing. In Belgium, 36% of consumers say that social media sometimes influence their purchases (Becom, 2026). For a small brand, social commerce is often the first international channel: a post seen in Cologne or Utrecht costs the same as a post seen in Namur.

The factory-to-consumer model. Temu and Shein have pushed a new logic: the platform directly connects Chinese factories and European consumers, without stock in Europe, with very low prices and lead times of one to two weeks. This model relies on sending millions of small parcels individually, long exempt from customs duty under 150 euros. It is precisely this exemption that the European Union removed on 1 July 2026, in favour of a 3-euro duty per item. The model is not dead, but its cost advantage has shrunk.

2.3 Own site or marketplace: the decision that shapes everything

For an e-merchant who wants to sell abroad, the first concrete decision is not the country: it is the channel. Three options, often combined.

The marketplace is an excellent entry channel, provided you plan for what comes next from the start.

2.5 The chapter’s tool: the Business Model Canvas

Alexander Osterwalder’s Business Model Canvas sums up a business model on one page in nine boxes. You probably came across it in the “Marketing fundamentals” unit; we use it here as the first building block of the business plan, because it forces you to answer this chapter’s questions: to whom do we sell, what, through which channel, and how do we make money.

The nine boxes, in the order in which it is useful to fill them in:

  1. Customer segments: who buys, in which countries.
  2. Value proposition: why they buy from you rather than elsewhere, market by market.
  3. Channels: own site, marketplaces, social media, points of sale.
  4. Customer relationship: personalisation, service, retention.
  5. Revenue streams: sales, subscriptions, commissions; by market.
  6. Key resources: that without which the model does not work.
  7. Key activities: what you do yourself.
  8. Key partners: what you entrust to others (logistics, payment, translation).
  9. Cost structure: the fixed and variable costs, including those specific to international.

For an international project, fill in one version per target market when the value proposition or the channels differ. Often, a single version with “DE” and “NL” tags in the relevant boxes is enough.

The BELGA CRAFT example: its Business Model Canvas

Three choices stand out from the canvas:

  • The model stays B2C and D2C: BELGA CRAFT sells on its own site, in three languages. But it adds Etsy as a test channel for Germany, because Etsy’s clientele is looking precisely for craft goods and the investment is low.
  • The value proposition is tailored by market: artisanal quality and pay-on-invoice for Germany, fast delivery and iDEAL for the Netherlands. These are hypotheses; they will be checked through interviews in module 2.
  • The costs specific to international already appear: translation, export advertising (15% of the target turnover), cross-border delivery and returns.

This canvas fits on one page and already contains half of milestone J1.

Multiple-choice questions

  1. In summary of chapters 3 and 4, why do segmentation (personas) and localisation go hand in hand in an international e-commerce project?

    Personas say who the customers are and what they expect; localisation translates these expectations into decisions about the website. At BELGA CRAFT, Lena's refusal to pay by card (persona) imposed PayPal and pay-on-invoice (localisation).

  2. A market shows high uncertainty avoidance (Hofstede) and has many "security-seeker buyers" (Klarna). Which combination of choices is the most consistent?

    The Klarna profile and the Hofstede dimension converge: a low tolerance for risk and a need for reassurance call for guarantees, trust marks, clear conditions, pay-on-invoice and a visible identity. Urgency and a playful tone are aimed at other profiles.

  3. Faced with a cart-to-order conversion rate four times lower on the German market (Lena's case), what is the right approach according to the module?

    A conversion gap rarely has a single cause. The module invites you to talk to customers (for lack of a budget) and to look at the concrete points: the moment of abandonment, the payment methods offered, the language of the reminders, the total price revealed at the end. "They do not like our jewellery" does not explain 61 carts created.

Who buys online, and why

3.1 Why segment online shoppers

A site that addresses everyone addresses no one. An e-merchant’s first decision is not technical: it is deciding who they are talking to. Marketing calls this segmentation; online, it has a particular feature: you have a great deal of data on visitors’ actual behaviour (where they come from, what they look at, where they drop off), but very little on who they are and what they want. Typologies and personas serve to fill that gap.

3.2 A starting typology: Klarna’s four profiles

Klarna’s study on the psychology of online shopping, carried out among Belgian consumers, crosses two axes: rational or emotional, modern or traditional. From this it derives four profiles.

Profile Who they are What they expect How they buy
Happy shoppers (modern, emotional) Curious, spontaneous, social Pleasure, novelty, discovery through social media Impulsive, happy to try an unknown brand
Smart shoppers (modern, rational) Organised, demanding Value for money, service, reliable delivery Compare, read reviews, use comparison sites
Wary conservatives (traditional, emotional) Anxious about online shopping Simplicity, reassurance Short journeys, need signs of trust
Security seekers (traditional, rational) Cautious, methodical Security, transparency, reliability Check everything, prefer well-known retailers

This typology is nothing scientific in the strict sense, and it is not the only one. It is useful for a practical reason: each profile calls for different responses on the site. A “happy shopper” wants novelty and visuals; a “security seeker” wants to see the company’s address, the VAT number, the labels, the return conditions and a payment method they know. A site that speaks only to the former loses the latter, and vice versa.

Common mistake. Taking a typology for a truth. The profiles are starting points. What counts is your own observations: the interviews from problem scenario 3 and, later, the data from your site.

3.3 What triggers or blocks an online purchase

Studies come and go and contradict one another on the percentages, but they agree on the drivers. Here they are, in the order the visitor encounters them.

Before even seeing the product: speed and mobile. A site that takes more than three seconds to display on a smartphone has already lost some of its visitors. This is not a matter of taste, it is measurable, and Google measures it for you (the PageSpeed Insights tool, presented in module 5).

On the product page: trust. Sharp and numerous photos, a precise description, reviews from identifiable customers, clear availability. For a foreign site, the question “who is behind this site?” is added: an address, an identifiable company, a telephone number or a chat that responds.

At the price stage: transparency. The visitor wants to know the total price before starting to fill in a form: product, delivery, taxes, any duties. A charge that appears at the last step is the leading cause of basket abandonment, in every survey, in every country.

At payment: familiarity. Each market has its preferred payment methods. A Dutch shopper looks for iDEAL; a German, PayPal or payment by invoice; a Belgian, Bancontact. Not offering it means asking the customer to trust a stranger with their bank card. Many refuse.

Around the purchase: delivery and returns. The announced lead time, the choice of location (home, pick-up point), tracking, and above all a readable returns policy. The fear of not being able to send back a disappointing product weighs more heavily for a purchase abroad.

Then: social proof and reward. Reviews, recommendations, influencers, loyalty programmes and personalised offers all bear on the decision and on repeat purchases.

And one driver to handle with care: urgency. “Only 2 left in stock”, “offer valid for 2 hours”. Effective, but regulated: false urgency is a misleading commercial practice under European law (see annex A).

3.4 Generations, countries and Chinese platforms

18-34 year-olds buy abroad and via social media more often; they discover brands on Instagram, TikTok and Pinterest rather than on Google. People over 50 buy less across borders, but with often higher baskets and more loyalty. Between neighbouring countries, the gaps are real: the Dutch are among the biggest online shoppers in Europe per capita; Germans return far more items than Belgians, especially in fashion.

Chinese platforms have changed the game: 62% of Belgians have already bought on Shein, Temu or a comparable platform, and just as many say they have run into a problem there (Becom, 2026). They have accustomed part of the consumer base to very low prices and highly engaging apps, but they have also created a demand for trust: knowing where the product comes from, who answers if there is a problem, whether standards are met. This is an opening for a European brand that plays the transparency card.

Finally, 29% of Belgian consumers already use an AI assistant to search for or compare products (Becom, 2026). The “I ask ChatGPT, then I click on the site it cites” journey now exists; it is covered in module 3.

3.5 The case of luxury and premium

For a piece of jewellery, a bag or a watch, the logic of price is partly reversed. The customer buys a story, a status, an experience, and the high price is part of the product. Scarcity (limited series, one-off pieces) increases desire instead of curbing it; storytelling justifies the value; the packaging, the handwritten card and the after-sales service are part of the purchase. Online, this translates into carefully produced photos and videos, the story of how it is made, and attention to the parcel as received. BELGA CRAFT plays in this category, even with an 85 euro average basket: it is craftsmanship, and the customer wants to know it.

3.6 The chapter’s tool: the persona

A persona is a fictional but realistic portrait of a typical customer, built from data (interviews, surveys, site statistics) and not from the imagination. It serves to make decisions: when you hesitate over a payment method, a button colour or a returns policy, you ask yourself what Lena would do.

A good persona fits on one page:

  • Identity: first name, age, city, occupation, situation.
  • Digital behaviour: devices, purchase frequency, where they discover brands, how they compare.
  • Motivations: why they would buy from you.
  • Barriers: what would stop them.
  • One quotation drawn from a real interview.
  • Their journey: from discovery to after-sales.

Two or three personas per market are enough. Beyond that, you no longer use them.

The BELGA CRAFT example: Lena, 34, Cologne

Lena was born out of six interviews conducted by BELGA CRAFT with potential German customers (two expatriates in Brussels, four Instagram contacts). Three elements came out of every interview: discovery through Pinterest and Instagram, the importance of the story of the materials, and the refusal to pay by card on an unfamiliar site. The last point directly changed the requirements specification: PayPal and payment by invoice (via Klarna) became mandatory for the German version.

The journey also shows where Lena may drop off at each stage. Each of these points becomes a requirement in the requirements specification (module 5) or an action in the marketing plan (module 3).

For your project. Your personas must come out of your interviews, not out of your head. If you have not yet spoken to a single potential customer in your target market, that is the priority for your independent hours this week.

4.1 Translating is not localising

To translate is to render a text in another language. To localise is to adapt the whole experience to a market: the language, but also the units, the date and price formats, the payment methods, the examples, the images, the humour, the colours, the reading direction, the commercial calendar’s holidays, and the tone. The two IKEA screenshots show it: beyond the language, the Saudi page reverses the navigation (right-to-left reading), puts promotions to the fore and displays different visuals.

For an SME, the full localisation of a site in three languages was prohibitively expensive ten years ago. Machine translation tools (DeepL, tools built into the platforms) and generative AI have changed the equation: the first version costs little. But proofreading by a native speaker remains essential, and it is this that makes the difference between a “translated” site and a site that people trust.

4.2 Hofstede’s six cultural dimensions

The Dutch psychologist Geert Hofstede proposed, on the basis of surveys conducted in dozens of countries, six dimensions for comparing national cultures. They are debated, sometimes criticised (they describe averages, not individuals, and are partly dated), but they remain the most widely used starting point for anticipating differences between markets.

Dimension What it measures What it changes on a site
Power distance Acceptance of inequalities of power High: recommendations from experts, authorities, celebrities. Low: peer reviews, egalitarian tone
Individualism Priority to the individual or to the group High: “you”, personalisation. Low: family, community, collective reviews
Motivation towards achievement (so-called “masculinity”) Competition and performance, or quality of life and consensus High: “the best”, rankings. Low: comfort, well-being, modesty
Uncertainty avoidance Tolerance of ambiguity and risk High: guarantees, labels, detailed conditions, payment after delivery. Low: less reassurance needed
Long-term orientation Immediate or deferred gratification High: durability, long-term savings. Low: promotions, novelty
Indulgence The place of pleasure and impulse High: playful tone, pleasure purchases. Low: sober tone, rational arguments

Read the chart for BELGA CRAFT. Belgium and Germany show high uncertainty avoidance: guarantees, clear returns and detailed conditions reassure. Germany and the Netherlands are more “indulgent” and less hierarchical than Belgium: a direct tone and reviews from ordinary customers work better than recommendations from experts. China, at the opposite end on almost everything, would require a completely rethought site: it is not a starting market for a Brussels SME.

Cross-cultural dimensions are explored in greater depth, from the standpoint of negotiation, in UE 715103U32D2. Here, they serve to formulate hypotheses about your markets, which your interviews will confirm or not.

4.3 The localisation points that matter most

Language. The majority of European consumers prefer to buy in their own language, and many give up on a site they understand poorly. Belgium illustrates this every day: a serious Belgian site exists at a minimum in French and in Dutch. Search keywords differ even between countries that share a language (you do not search for the same terms in Belgium and in France), which has consequences for search engine ranking (module 3).

Payment methods. The table below is to be known by heart. It is detailed in module 4.

Country What the customer expects to find

Netherlands iDEAL above all, then cards and Klarna

Visual codes. Red brings luck in China and signals danger or sales in Europe; white is the colour of mourning in several Asian countries. Information density varies: uncluttered pages in Northern Europe, very busy ones in China and Japan. Visuals should look like the country’s customers.

The calendar. Christmas and the sales do not fall on the same dates everywhere; Black Friday has taken hold in Europe; Singles’ Day (11 November) dominates in China; Mother’s Day is not the same Sunday in Belgium, France and Germany. Your promotional calendar must be local.

Trust statements. A German consumer looks for the Impressum (detailed legal notices, mandatory in Germany) and labels such as Trusted Shops; a Dutch consumer, the Thuiswinkel Waarborg label; a Belgian, the Becom label or the company’s physical address.

4.4 Three documented adaptation failures

Amazon in China (closure of the marketplace in 2019). Having entered China in 2004 through the acquisition of Joyo, Amazon never rose above a marginal market share against Taobao and JD.com. Its sober interface, its classic customer reviews and its recommendation model did not match local habits: entertainment, permanent promotions, live video selling, influencers (KOL), instant messaging between buyer and seller. The lesson is simple: global power is no substitute for understanding a market.

eBay in China (withdrawal in 2006). The global leader in C2C, eBay was beaten by Taobao, which made selling free for sellers and added messaging between buyer and seller, meeting a need for negotiation and direct contact. Here, it was not the site that was poorly adapted, but the business model.

IKEA in Saudi Arabia (2012). The Saudi catalogue had erased women from the photos of the international catalogue. The outcry in Sweden forced IKEA to apologise. Localisation must not betray the brand’s values, nor the expectations of its home market: an adaptation visible in one country is visible in all the others too.

Common mistake. Illustrating cultural adaptation with unverifiable anecdotes (the Pepsi slogan that “brings ancestors back to life”, the Nova name that “doesn’t go” in Spanish). They are amusing, often false, and teach you nothing. Prefer documented cases, with sources.

4.5 Choosing your markets: the attractiveness-feasibility matrix

Before localising anything, you have to choose where. The simplest method is a two-axis matrix.

Market attractiveness: the size of the online market in your category, growth, purchasing power, the share of consumers who already buy abroad, the intensity of competition.

Feasibility for you: language (do you master it?), distance and delivery cost, payment methods to integrate, regulatory complexity (EU or non-EU), cultural affinity, existing contacts.

Score each criterion from 1 to 5, weight them, and compare at least four markets. The right answer for an SME is rarely “the biggest market”: it is the biggest market among those it can serve properly.

Multiple-choice questions

  1. An SME translates its Belgian homepage into German in one afternoon, changing nothing else (same photos, same slogan, Bancontact as the first payment method). What is it missing to truly address the German market?

Open questions

  1. Explain the difference between translating and localising, then cite five elements of a website that are a matter of localisation without being translation.

    Translating means rendering a text into another language. Localising means adapting the whole experience to a market: language is only part of the work. Five elements that are a matter of localisation without being translation: payment methods (iDEAL in the Netherlands, PayPal and pay-on-invoice in Germany, Bancontact in Belgium); trust mentions and labels (Impressum and Trusted Shops in Germany, Thuiswinkel Waarborg in the Netherlands); visual codes and information density (clean pages in the North, colours whose meaning changes from country to country); the promotional calendar (Black Friday, Singles' Day, Mother's Day on different dates); and the reading direction and navigation (right to left in Arabic, as on IKEA's Saudi website). You can add units, date and price formats, and tone. For an SME, machine translation makes the first version inexpensive, but it is proofreading by a native speaker that makes the difference between a "translated" website and one that is trusted.

  2. The three adaptation failures documented by the course (Amazon and eBay in China, IKEA in Saudi Arabia) do not teach the same lesson. Explain the lesson of each.

    Amazon in China shows that global power does not replace an understanding of a market: its plain interface, its classic reviews and its recommendations did not match local habits (entertainment, permanent promotions, live video selling, KOL influencers, buyer-seller messaging), and Amazon never exceeded a marginal share against Taobao and JD.com. eBay in China shows that a failure can come from the business model and not from the localisation of the website: Taobao made selling free for sellers and added messaging that met the need for negotiation and direct contact. IKEA in Saudi Arabia (the 2012 catalogue that erased women from the photos) shows that localisation must not betray the brand's values or the expectations of its home market: an adaptation visible in one country is visible everywhere too, and the outcry in Sweden forced IKEA to apologise. Finally, the course warns against unverifiable anecdotes (Pepsi, Nova): documented and sourced cases are preferred.

  3. A Liège e-commerce retailer of specialty beers is hesitating between the Netherlands, France, Italy and the United States. Build the reasoning of an attractiveness-feasibility matrix and say which markets you would select.

Feasibility: a mastered language, distance and delivery cost from Liège, payment methods to integrate, regulatory complexity (EU or non-EU, and excise duties on alcohol), cultural affinity, existing contacts. You score each criterion from 1 to 5, weight them and compare the four markets. The Netherlands comes out on top: proximity, fast and inexpensive delivery, iDEAL simple to integrate, an EU market with no customs border, a strong online-buying culture. France is attractive because of its size and the absence of a language barrier, but competition there is strong on beer. Italy adds the language barrier and longer logistics. The United States, a huge market, combines customs, VAT/excise, alcohol regulation by state and distant delivery: attractive but barely feasible for a beginning SME. I would therefore select the Netherlands and France, keeping in mind that the right choice is not "the biggest market" but the biggest among those you can serve properly.

5.1 Product: standardise, adapt or create

Three strategies, and an SME often combines them.

Strategy Principle Examples When
Standardisation The same product everywhere Apple, Lego, a design brand Universal product, strong brand image, economies of scale
Adaptation The same product, adjusted Decathlon (ranges by climate), clothing sizes, electrical plugs Local constraints, different tastes
Local creation A product specific to a market Asian ranges of major cosmetics groups Large market, very different expectations

Four families of factors push towards adapting: regulation (safety standards, labelling in the country’s language, the European regulation on product safety, known as the GPSR, in force since December 2024), culture (tastes, sizes, colours, uses), the economy (purchasing power, positioning) and the climate (reversed seasons, conditions of use).

For BELGA CRAFT, the product stays standard: a piece of jewellery handmade in Brussels is precisely what the German customer is buying. The adaptation bears on the augmented product: the packaging (a card in German telling the story of how it is made), the personalisation (engraving, choice of stone), the guarantee.

5.2 Price: four strategies and three rules

Strategy Description Advantages Disadvantages
Uniform price The same price excl. tax everywhere Simple, consistent, no arbitrage Ignores purchasing power and local competition
Localised price One price per market Competitive everywhere Complex to manage, customers who compare
Skimming High price at launch Margin, image Low volumes
Penetration Low price to get in Fast volumes Low margin, hard to raise

Three rules specific to cross-border online selling:

1. The customer compares the landed price, not the displayed price. Product price, plus delivery, plus the VAT of their country, plus any duties outside the EU. A product price lower than the competition is useless if delivery costs 15 euros more. The calculation of the landed price is in module 4.

2. The European regulation against unjustified geo-blocking (2018) frames price differences within the EU. You can have a .de site with prices different from the .be site. But you cannot prevent a Belgian customer from buying on the .de site on German terms, nor forcibly redirect them to the Belgian site. You are not obliged to deliver to Belgium from the German site; the customer can collect the item or arrange delivery themselves. In practice, significant price gaps between neighbouring countries always end up becoming known and upsetting people.

3. Any announcement of a reduction must indicate, as the reference price, the lowest price applied over the preceding 30 days (Omnibus directive, in force since 2022). Remember Amazon’s “-50%” in chapter 1, calculated on the manufacturer’s catalogue price: the exact wording matters, and the FPS Economy checks.

Common mistake. Setting foreign prices by simply converting the Belgian price. Psychological prices differ (9.99 in France, 9.95 in the Netherlands), VAT differs (21% in Belgium and the Netherlands, 19% in Germany, 20% in France), and so do payment and delivery charges. Start from the landed price you want to see displayed, and work back up.

5.3 Distribution: where to sell

The channels were presented in chapter 2; here is how to combine them by market.

  • Your own site: full control of the brand and the data, traffic to win. Essential in the long run.
  • Marketplaces: Amazon (everywhere), bol (Benelux), Zalando (fashion, Europe), Etsy (crafts, worldwide), Cdiscount (France), Allegro (Poland), Otto (Germany). Immediate audience, commissions, dependence.
  • Social media: Instagram, TikTok Shop, Pinterest (strong for decoration and jewellery).
  • Local partners: independent boutiques (via Ankorstore, for example), concept stores, specialised online resellers.

The practical rule: per market, one main channel and one secondary channel, chosen according to your personas. For Lena, it is Pinterest and Instagram for discovery, Etsy for the trial, your own site for loyalty.

5.4 Promotion: adapt the message and choose the channels

Three levels of message adaptation: translation (same message, another language), transposition (same idea, local execution: visuals, references, humour) and creation (a different message per market). Most SMEs stick to transposition.

The dominant channels vary: Google dominates search in Europe and the Americas, Baidu in China, Naver in South Korea, Yandex in Russia; Instagram and TikTok dominate among the under-35s, Facebook remains strong beyond; LinkedIn is the B2B channel. Email remains the most profitable channel for existing customers, provided you have their consent (GDPR).

Two indicators to steer by: the customer acquisition cost (CAC: acquisition spend divided by the number of new customers) and the return on ad spend (ROAS: turnover generated per euro of advertising). A CAC only makes sense compared to the value of the customer (point 6.4).

6.3 Convincing: conversion

The conversion rate is the share of visitors who buy. In B2C e-commerce, it is often between 1 and 3%, more for loyal customers, less for cold traffic coming from advertising. Internationally, four factors weigh more than elsewhere:

  • Local trust: reviews, the country’s labels, complete legal notices, address, reachable contact.
  • Familiar payments: the absence of the expected payment method is one of the leading causes of abandonment at the last step.
  • Language: right down to confirmation emails and customer service.
  • The total price visible early: delivery charges and taxes announced from the product page or the basket onwards.

The method for making progress is the A/B test: two versions of a page, shown at random to two halves of the visitors, and you keep the one that converts best. Internationally, test by country: what works in Germany may fail in Italy. The elements to test as a priority: the order and presentation of payment methods, reassurance messages, the length of forms, the call-to-action button, the basket page.

6.4 Bringing them back: loyalty

Acquiring a customer is expensive; bringing them back costs little. This is why the customer lifetime value (CLV: the total margin a customer brings over their whole relationship with you) is the indicator that justifies, or not, your acquisition spend. The rule of thumb: a CLV/CAC ratio of at least 3.

7.1 What the customer expects, and what it costs

The online customer expects four things from delivery: an announced and honoured delivery time, a choice of location (home, pickup point, locker), tracking and easy returns. The big platforms have set the standard (next-day delivery at bol and Coolblue, free returns at Zalando), and an SME must position itself against it, without necessarily copying it.

Every promise has a cost. A parcel of under 2 kg to Germany or the Netherlands costs, for a small SME without a negotiated rate, in the order of 7 to 12 euros; a return as much again. In fashion, returns can affect a third of orders; in Germany, more. The Torfs case (appendix B) shows a profitable Belgian retailer whose online margin was eroded by the shipping and return costs it chose to offer. These costs must appear in your business plan from the first version.

7.2 Three ways to deliver to a foreign customer

Direct shipping: each order leaves Brussels via bpost, PostNL, DHL, DPD or GLS. This is the starting point for any SME: simple, with no investment, but more expensive per parcel and slower, with returns that come back to Belgium.

Direct injection: parcels for the same country are grouped and handed over in one batch to that country’s distribution network (by a carrier or a consolidator). The cost per parcel falls and the delivery time approaches domestic levels, provided you have a regular volume, at least a few dozen parcels a week.

Stock in the country: a local logistics provider, or a marketplace’s logistics (Amazon, bol), stores your products and ships within one or two days; returns stay local. This is the model for high volumes; it ties up stock and creates fixed costs. For a made-to-order product like BELGA CRAFT’s, it only applies to the common items.

The three models often follow one another over the life of an e-commerce business, at the pace of volumes.

7.3 Procurement and stock: the essentials

Even though your supply chain course goes further, remember four things for the business plan.

  • Procurement strategies: centralised (everything comes from one country), “China + 1” (a second source outside China), nearshoring (production brought closer: Eastern Europe, Turkey, Morocco), decentralised (several warehouses). Since the crises of 2020–2024, many companies have brought part of their production closer.
  • Demand forecasting per market, with different seasonalities (Christmas, Mother’s Day, Black Friday, sales).
  • Safety stock, which depends on demand variability and the procurement lead time.
  • The indicators: stockout rate, service rate, stock turnover, days of cover. For accounting valuation, the “first in, first out” method (FIFO) is the reference; the LIFO method is prohibited under IFRS.

7.4 VAT and customs: what every e-commerce business must know

Sale to a consumer in another EU country. As long as your distance sales to all other EU countries combined stay under 10,000 euros a year, you apply Belgian VAT. Above that, you apply the customer’s country’s VAT (19% in Germany, 21% in the Netherlands, 20% in France…). So as not to register in each country, you declare and pay these VAT amounts in a single quarterly return via the OSS one-stop shop, with the FPS Finance. Practical consequence: your site must be able to display and invoice different VAT rates depending on the delivery country.

Sale to a consumer outside the EU (Switzerland, United Kingdom, United States…). You do not charge Belgian VAT (export). But on arrival, the customer’s country collects its VAT, any customs duties and customs-clearance fees charged by the carrier. Two options: delivered duty paid (DDP), where you pay everything in advance and build it into your price, or duties at the customer’s charge (DAP), where the customer pays on delivery. The second option is acceptable if it is clearly announced; it is disastrous when it is discovered at the door, as in the opening question.

Goods imported from third countries sold to EU consumers (parcels of 150 euros maximum). Since July 2021, VAT is due on all parcels, even low-value ones; the IOSS one-stop shop lets you collect it at the time of sale, so that the customer has nothing to pay on delivery. Since 1 July 2026, the duty exemption below 150 euros has disappeared: a flat duty of 3 euros per item applies. This reform targets primarily the platforms that ship from China, but it concerns any seller whose customers have parcels imported.

The documents. A shipment outside the EU travels with a commercial invoice and a customs declaration (CN22 or CN23 for the post) stating the product’s nomenclature code (HS code), which determines the applicable duties. Carriers generate these documents if you provide them with the right data from your site: this is a requirement of the requirements specification.

Incoterms, customs regimes and VAT in detail are dealt with in UE 719105U32D2.

7.5 The landed price: the central concept of the chapter

The landed price (landed cost) is what the customer really pays: the product price, plus delivery, plus their country’s VAT, plus any customs duties, plus customs-clearance fees. It is this price that the customer compares with your competitors’, and it is this price that your site must display before payment, or at least announce.

The BELGA CRAFT example: three landed-price calculations

Case 1 — German customer, jewellery displayed at 95 euros incl. VAT on the German site. BELGA CRAFT exceeds 10,000 euros of distance sales in the EU: it applies German VAT of 19%, declared via the OSS. Price excluding VAT: 95 ÷ 1.19 = €79.83. Delivery free from 80 euros. No customs. Landed price: 95 euros, the one displayed. Nothing to pay on delivery.

Case 2 — Swiss customer, jewellery displayed at 80 euros excluding VAT. Export outside the EU, so no Belgian VAT. Delivery 12 euros. On arrival: Swiss VAT of 8.1% on the goods and the transport, i.e. €7.45. Customs duties: Switzerland abolished duties on industrial products on 1 January 2024, so €0. Carrier’s customs-clearance fees: variable, here €12. Landed price: 111.45 euros, of which 19.45 euros demanded at the door if BELGA CRAFT sells on a DAP basis. On a DDP basis, BELGA CRAFT pays these 19.45 euros and displays an all-inclusive price. The opening question is settled: either display the landed price, or warn in full words.

Case 3 — Belgian customer of a Chinese seller, item at 40 euros. Belgian VAT of 21% collected at the sale if the seller or the platform uses IOSS (€8.40). Since 1 July 2026: flat duty of 3 euros per item. Landed price: 51.40 euros excluding delivery. This case concerns you as a competitor: the price gap with the Chinese platforms has narrowed.

Common mistake. Displaying “delivery: €12” and believing the Swiss customer is warned. She is, for delivery, but not for VAT and customs-clearance fees. The notice must be explicit: “Outside the EU: VAT, duties and customs-clearance fees at the recipient’s charge, approximately X% of the amount.”

  1. Explain the notion of "landed price" (landed cost), list its components and say why it is the central notion of the chapter for anyone selling abroad.

    The landed price is what the customer actually pays once the parcel is delivered: (1) the price of the product, (2) the delivery fees, (3) the VAT of his country, (4) any customs duties (sales outside the EU), (5) the customs-clearance fees charged by the carrier. It is this price - and not the price displayed excluding tax - that the customer compares with your competitors. It is central because an attractive entry price can become uncompetitive, or even trigger a parcel refusal and a negative review, if fees appear on delivery. The website must therefore display the landed price before payment, or failing that explicitly announce the remaining fees (e.g. for Switzerland: "Outside the EU: VAT, duties and customs-clearance fees borne by the recipient").

  2. Describe the three ways to deliver a foreign customer, with for each its advantages, its drawbacks and the moment in the life of the company when it is suitable.

    1) Direct shipping: each order leaves from Brussels (bpost, PostNL, DHL, DPD, GLS). Simple, with no investment, it is the starting point for any SME; but more expensive per parcel, slower, and returns come back to Belgium. 2) Direct injection: the parcels of a single country are grouped and handed over in one go to the national distribution network. The cost per parcel falls and the delivery time approaches the domestic one; but it requires a regular volume (a few dozen parcels/week). 3) Stock in the country (a local provider or a marketplace's logistics): shipping in 1-2 days, local returns; but this ties up stock and creates fixed costs - reserved for large volumes and, for a made-to-order product, for the common pieces only. The three models often follow one another as volumes grow.

  3. BELGA CRAFT sells 12,000 euros per year to Dutch customers and 3,000 euros to German customers. Which VAT does it apply to each, and how does it declare it?

    The total of intra-EU distance sales (12,000 + 3,000 = 15,000 euros) exceeds the 10,000-euro threshold. It therefore applies the VAT of the customer's country for both: 21% for Dutch customers, 19% for German customers. To avoid registering for VAT in each country, it declares and pays this VAT in a single quarterly declaration via the OSS one-stop shop to the SPF Finances. Its website must be able to display and invoice different VAT rates depending on the country of delivery.

8.4 Security: threats and protections

The threats: payment fraud (stolen cards, identity theft, abusive chargebacks), phishing (fake sites or fake emails imitating your brand), data leaks (theft of the customer database), and money laundering (use of your shop for illicit transactions).

The protections:

  • HTTPS across the whole site, not just the payment page.
  • Strong customer authentication (SCA), imposed in the EU by the PSD2 directive and implemented through 3-D Secure 2: the customer confirms the payment via their banking app or a code. It reduces fraud and shifts the liability for disputes to the bank.
  • Tokenisation: your site never stores a card number; the gateway replaces it with a token.
  • PCI DSS compliance, carried in practice by the gateway if you do not handle the card data yourself.
  • The gateways’ anti-fraud tools, which score each transaction in real time (address, device, history).

In the event of a personal data leak, the GDPR requires notifying the Data Protection Authority (DPA) within 72 hours and informing the individuals concerned if the risk is high. A written reaction plan prepared in advance, even a single page, is worth more than improvising on a Black Friday Saturday.

(3) Cart and payment - total price visible with VAT and fees, payment methods offered by country, possibility of buying without an account, number of steps. (4) Reassurance - reviews, legal notices, contact channels. You score each step out of 5. Finally, you distinguish what is costly to copy (e.g. 4,000 reviews) from what is not (displaying the delivery time on the product page) in order to prioritise what you will take up.

Opening question

10.1 What a requirements specification is for

The requirements specification describes what the site must do, for whom, with which constraints, in what timeframe and what budget. It serves three purposes: to obtain comparable quotes, to frame the build (what is not in it will not be delivered), and to check on delivery that you have what you asked for. It does not say how to do it: that is the provider’s job.

The pedagogical dossier makes it the second learning outcome and sets seven minimum sections. They follow the customer’s journey.

10.2 The seven sections, one by one

1. Ergonomics. The tree structure (categories, depth), navigation and search, the mobile version (designed first for the smartphone), accessibility: since 28 June 2025, e-commerce sites aimed at consumers must be accessible to people with disabilities (European Accessibility Act; reference standard EN 301 549, which incorporates the WCAG criteria). Micro-enterprises benefit from a special regime to be checked.

2. Language adaptation. The languages per market, what is translated and what is localised, who translates and who reviews, the management of content in several languages (one page, three versions), SEO per language (site structure, hreflang, keywords).

3. Welcoming visitors. The home page per country, language detection (offer, never force a redirect: this is also a rule of the anti-geoblocking regulation), the reassurance elements visible from arrival (reviews, labels, address, contact), the compliant cookie banner (refusing as simple as accepting).

4. Loyalty. The customer account (optional at purchase), the order history, the programme chosen in module 3 (points, referral…), the automated emails (confirmation, shipping, review request, basket reminder) with consent, review collection.

5. Basket. The total price displayed with the country’s VAT and the delivery costs, basket saving, promotional codes, free-delivery thresholds, abandoned-basket reminders, delivery-time estimate.

6. Purchasing process. The number of steps (one page or several), purchase without creating an account, the payment methods per country and their display order, strong authentication, the confirmation page, the invoice (structured electronic invoice for Belgian business customers since 2026).

7. Order tracking. Notifications by email or SMS, the tracking number, the choice of delivery point, the returns procedure (label, drop-off point, refund within 14 days), and the online withdrawal button, mandatory since 19 June 2026 for distance sales to consumers: a clearly identified function, available throughout the withdrawal period, followed by a confirmation on a durable medium.

10.3 The full structure of the document

  1. Presentation of the company and the project
  2. Objectives: markets, target turnover, timelines, success indicators
  3. Existing situation: current site, tools (stock, accounting, CRM), content to carry over, constraints
  4. Functional specifications: the seven sections, front-office and back-office (management of orders, stock, content, returns)
  5. Technical specifications: platform, hosting, expected performance, integrations (payment gateway, carriers, electronic invoicing, accounting), security, backups
  6. Design: graphic charter, mock-ups of the key pages (home, category, product, basket, payment, tracking)
  7. Legal obligations: GDPR and cookies, legal notices per country, consumer law, accessibility, VAT per country, product safety (GPSR information on the product pages)
  8. Planning: phases, milestones, tests, go-live per market
  9. Budget: creation, subscriptions, maintenance, translations, developments
  10. Expected response arrangements from providers

11.4 The revenue model and the hypotheses

For each market and each year:

Turnover = visitors × conversion rate × average basket, adjusted for seasonality and repeat purchase.

Variable costs: purchase or manufacture of the products, marketplace commissions, payment fees, delivery, returns, packaging. Fixed costs: staff, subscriptions (platform, apps, gateway), basic marketing, translations, accounting, insurance.

Open questions

  1. The banker asks: "What happens if your acquisition cost doubles?" How do you build your answer from your financial model?

In the financial model file, you double the acquisition-cost line (the marketing per order) and look at three figures: (1) the contribution margin per market, which shrinks; (2) the result of the export activity, which may swing back into a loss; (3) the cash low point and the financing need, which increase.   2. Calculate the break-even point of an activity whose fixed costs are 70,000 euros and whose contribution margin is 46% of turnover excluding VAT. Then explain what this figure means.

    Break-even point = fixed costs / contribution-margin rate = 70,000 / 0.46 = about 152,174 euros of turnover excluding VAT. Meaning: as long as the turnover excluding VAT is below about 152,000 euros, the margin generated on sales (46% of every euro sold) is not enough to cover the 70,000 euros of fixed costs and the activity is at a loss; beyond that, every additional euro of turnover generates 0.46 euros that contributes to the result. It is this level of sales that must be aimed at and dated (which month, which year) in the business plan, and it is this that is compared with the turnover forecast in the cautious and central scenarios to judge the project's credibility.

You therefore present three scenarios - cautious, central, optimistic - each with its costed and sourced assumptions (sector benchmark, test, current Belgian figures, quotes). You adopt the financing need of the cautious scenario because, if it is underestimated, the company finds itself out of cash at the worst moment. Self-assessment

Test yourself before moving on. Multiple-choice questions are marked with one click; for open questions, write your answer first, then compare it with the model answer.

Multiple-choice questions

  1. BELGA CRAFT sells its jewellery directly to private individuals via its own website. Which business model does this sale mainly belong to (module 1)?

    Selling from a company to consumers is B2C (business to consumer). B2B targets other companies, C2C exchanges between private individuals, B2G sales to administrations. Structured electronic invoicing (Peppol) appears, moreover, only for Belgian business customers, that is, the B2B side.

  2. The guiding thread of the course requires a business project that wants to sell online in how many foreign markets?

    From the first session, each project chooses a company that wants to sell in at least two foreign markets. This is what makes the course "international" and justifies milestone J2 (choice of the two markets) and all the work of localisation, logistics and payment by country.

  3. Why should you never forcibly redirect a visitor to the version of his country (modules 2 and 5)?

    Language detection must offer, never forcibly redirect: this is a rule of the anti-geoblocking regulation, recalled in the "welcoming visitors" section of the requirements specification. A customer must be able to stay on the version he has chosen.

  4. What is the difference between translating and localising a website (module 2)?

    Translating covers only the language; localising goes further - currency, taxes, calendar, content and the market's habits. The audit grid (chapter 9) distinguishes, moreover, "language, currency, taxes, content, calendar adapted" (successful localisation) from mere "translation only".

  5. In the 4P applied to international e-commerce (module 3), what does the "P" for place (distribution) cover?

    Learning outcome 1 specifies that the 4P are product, price, promotion, and place (distribution and logistics) - this last P explicitly including payment gateways. Price, promotion and product are the three other P's.

  6. What does the customer acquisition cost (CAC), a key indicator of the web-marketing strategy (module 3), measure?

    The CAC is what it costs, on average, to acquire a new customer (advertising, marketing). It is one of the indicators to track by market, and it is one of the assumptions the banker tests ("what happens if the CAC doubles?"). The average basket, the repeat-purchase rate and the margin are other, distinct indicators.

  7. To sell in the Netherlands, which payment method must feature prominently in the checkout funnel (module 4)?

    The extract from BELGA CRAFT's requirements specification orders the methods by country: in the Netherlands, iDEAL comes first, then cards, Klarna, PayPal. Bancontact is the Belgian method, and offering the right local methods by country strongly determines conversion.

  8. For a delivery outside the European Union, what must the website mention explicitly (module 4)?

    The extract from the requirements specification provides, for destinations outside the EU, an explicit mention of the duties and fees borne by the recipient. Omitting this information exposes the customer to a surprise surcharge on delivery and generates disputes and returns.

Open questions

  1. The jury asks you to prove the CONSISTENCY of your file: "Show the link between your choice of markets (J2), your payment strategy (J4) and your financial forecasts (chapter 11)." How do you respond?

    I unfold the chain, piece by piece. J2: I selected Germany and the Netherlands for reasoned reasons (size, proximity, personas). This determines J4: for these two countries I offer the right local payment methods - iDEAL leading in the Netherlands, PayPal and Klarna invoice in Germany, via a gateway (Mollie) - because a bad payment choice makes conversion drop. These same markets and these payment fees are found line by line in the financial model: visitors, conversion rate and average basket per country give the turnover, and the payment fees (2.4% in Germany, 1.0% in the Netherlands in the example) enter the variable costs. The proof of consistency is that no market promised in J2 is missing from the forecasts and no payment method promised in J4 is absent from the requirements specification. This is exactly the inconsistency that BELGA CRAFT had had to correct (the Dutch market absent from the forecasts, iDEAL absent from the requirements specification).

  2. You have 15 minutes to present before the jury. What structure do you adopt, and what mistake must you absolutely avoid?

    A seven-part structure, aligned with what the jury expects: (1) the project and its value proposition; (2) the markets chosen and why; (3) the customers (personas, what the interviews showed); (4) the web-marketing strategy (4P, acquisition, loyalty); (5) operations (delivery, returns, customs, payments); (6) the figures (scenarios, break-even point, cash low point); (7) the financing need. The mistake to avoid absolutely: reading out the business plan. You tell a story carried by a few key figures, you do not recite the document. Other pitfalls: going over time (I rehearse out loud, stopwatch in hand, because 15 minutes go by fast), and hiding a fragile assumption - better to flag it yourself and say what you would do if it did not hold. I bring a printed version of the file in case the projector breaks down.

  3. The jury asks: "What is your main risk, and how do you reduce it?" Give a reasoned example for an international e-commerce project.

    I name a real risk among those that section 11.3 identifies internationally: dependence on a marketplace or a gateway. Example: if 40% of my German sales go through Etsy in the first year, a change in Etsy's conditions or commissions can eat into my margin overnight - this is, moreover, one of the jury's typical questions. How I reduce it: I use Etsy to acquire my first customers, but I gradually shift traffic to my own website in German (launched in July of year 1 in the example), I capture customers' email for loyalty, and I let the marketplace carry only a decreasing share of the turnover. I can cite other risks depending on the project (currency outside the euro zone, VAT/accessibility compliance, cross-border delivery delays), but the jury expects above all that I show I have a costed reduction plan, not just a list of fears.

The legal framework of e-commerce in brief

This appendix gives the essential reference points for the business plan (section 9) and the requirements specification (section 7). International trade law is covered in more depth in course unit 713009U32D2. The rules change fast: always check the version in force on official websites (FPS Economy, FPS Finance, Data Protection Authority, European Commission).

A.1 Data protection

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, 2018) applies as soon as you process the data of people located in the EU, and it is exported: most major markets have adopted comparable laws (United Kingdom, Switzerland, Brazil, California, China). For an e-retailer, this means:

  • a legal basis for each processing operation (the contract to fulfil the order, consent for advertising);
  • a clear privacy policy, in the customer’s language;
  • respect for individuals’ rights: access, rectification, erasure, portability, objection;
  • a register of processing activities and proportionate security measures;
  • non-essential cookies subject to prior consent, with refusal being as easy as acceptance;
  • the notification of a data breach to the Data Protection Authority within 72 hours, and informing the individuals if the risk is high.

Penalties: up to 20 million euros or 4% of worldwide turnover.

A.2 VAT and customs

  • July 2021 reform: end of the VAT exemption for small imported parcels; a single threshold of 10,000 euros for distance sales within the EU, above which the VAT of the customer’s country applies; the OSS (intra-EU sales) and IOSS (imports of 150 euros maximum) one-stop shops.
  • 2026 customs reform: since 1 July 2026, a flat duty of 3 euros per item on parcels worth less than 150 euros imported into the EU, replacing the exemption.
  • Electronic invoicing: in Belgium, mandatory between VAT-registered businesses since 1 January 2026, in a structured format via the Peppol network (Royal Decree of 14 July 2025). Sales to consumers are not affected. Fines of 1,500 to 5,000 euros per breach.

A.3 Consumer protection

  • Pre-contractual information: identity and address of the seller, product features, total price, delivery terms, right of withdrawal, two-year legal guarantee.
  • Right of withdrawal: 14 days for distance purchases, refund within 14 days; exceptions (made-to-measure or personalised goods, perishable goods, digital content, etc.).
  • Online withdrawal button: mandatory since 19 June 2026 (Directive (EU) 2023/2673): a clearly identified function, available throughout the withdrawal period, a form, a confirmation, an acknowledgement of receipt on a durable medium. Without information about its existence, the withdrawal period is extended by twelve months.
  • Price-reduction announcements: the reference price is the lowest price applied over the previous 30 days (Omnibus Directive, 2022).
  • Customer reviews: the site must state whether it verifies that reviews come from genuine buyers; fake reviews and paid-for reviews are prohibited.
  • Misleading practices: false urgency, fake countdowns, pre-ticked boxes and “dark patterns” are unfair commercial practices.
  • Geo-blocking: prohibition on discriminating against EU customers based on their nationality or place of residence (Regulation (EU) 2018/302); no obligation to deliver everywhere, but no refusal to sell and no forced redirection.
  • Applicable law: a consumer cannot be deprived of the mandatory protection of the law of their country of residence, as soon as the site targets that country.

A.4 Platforms and marketplaces

  • Digital Services Act (DSA), applicable to all platforms since 17 February 2024: traceability of professional sellers on marketplaces, procedures for reporting illegal content and products, transparency of advertising and recommendation systems, prohibition of targeted advertising based on the profiling of minors or on sensitive data. Reinforced obligations for very large platforms (more than 45 million users in the EU), including Amazon, AliExpress, Temu and Shein.
  • DAC7: platforms transmit to the tax authorities the data of sellers who exceed 30 sales or 2,000 euros per year.
  • “Platform to business” Regulation (2019): marketplaces owe clear terms and conditions and advance notice of any restriction or suspension of a professional seller.

A.5 Products

  • General product safety (Regulation (EU) 2023/988, known as the GPSR, applicable since 13 December 2024): mandatory online information (manufacturer, responsible person in the EU, warnings, product identifier), obligations for marketplaces, recalls.
  • Sector-specific regulations: cosmetics (CPNP notification, labelling), toys (CE marking), electronics (CE, equipment waste, batteries), textiles (fibre labelling), food (allergens, labelling in the country’s language), etc.
  • Packaging: contributions to recycling schemes in each country (Fost Plus in Belgium; specific obligations in Germany via the LUCID register).

A.6 Accessibility

The European Accessibility Act (Directive (EU) 2019/882) has applied since 28 June 2025 to e-commerce services aimed at consumers: websites and applications must be usable by people with disabilities (reference standard EN 301 549, which incorporates the WCAG criteria). In Belgium, enforcement falls to the FPS Economy; micro-enterprises (fewer than ten people and less than two million euros in turnover) benefit from a special regime to be verified. Penalties provided for in Belgium: fines of up to 200,000 euros or 6% of turnover.

A.7 Artificial intelligence

The European Artificial Intelligence Regulation (AI Act, 2024) classifies systems by level of risk. Product recommendations, dynamic pricing or the chatbots of an online store do not, in principle, fall under “high risk”. What concerns e-commerce most are the transparency obligations, applicable since 2 August 2026: informing people that they are interacting with an AI system, and flagging AI-generated content. The “Digital Omnibus” adopted in 2026 postponed the obligations relating to high-risk systems to December 2027 and August 2028.

A.8 Intellectual property

  • Protect your brand in the targeted countries: European Union trademark (EUIPO) or national filings; check availability before launching a site in a new country.
  • Monitor counterfeits on marketplaces and use their brand protection programmes.
  • Make sure you have the rights to the photos, texts, fonts and music you use; an image “found on the internet” is not free to use.

Ten companies, ten lessons

Ten companies — Belgian, European and global: spectacular failures, consolidations, and genuine successes. For each case, the facts, then what they mean for your project. The figures come from the cited and dated public sources; check them before reusing them.

B.1 Groupon: growing too fast in too many countries

Sector: local discount vouchers (restaurants, leisure, services). Model: a platform between businesses and consumers, earning a high commission on each voucher sold. Period: since 2008.

The rise. Founded in 2008 in Chicago by Andrew Mason on a simple idea: a deal of the day at a slashed price, valid only if a minimum number of buyers commit. In 2010, Groupon bought the German company CityDeal to establish itself in Europe and refused a takeover offer from Google of around 6 billion dollars. In November 2011, the stock market flotation at 20 dollars per share valued the company at around 12.7 billion dollars.

International expansion. In two years, Groupon opened or bought sites in dozens of countries. The local teams were poorly integrated, the technical platforms differed from one country to another, and in several markets, China foremost among them, better-adapted local competitors already dominated.

The weaknesses of the model. For the retailer, a half-price discount plus a commission of around half the remaining price often left a loss; many customers attracted by the voucher never came back at the normal price; and the concept, with no barrier to entry, was copied everywhere.

The decline. Andrew Mason was dismissed in February 2013. Groupon gradually withdrew from many countries, tried product sales (Groupon Goods, abandoned in 2020) and laid off around 2,800 people in spring 2020.

The recent situation. In 2025, Groupon generated 498 million dollars in turnover (compared with around 1.6 billion in 2011), with a net loss of 81 million and 16.2 million active customers. Management speaks of a return to growth “for the first time in ten years”.

For your project. Do not open more markets than you can manage. A model must be profitable for all parties, here the retailers. An acquisition without technical and human integration destroys value.

Sources: Associated Press and Christian Science Monitor, November 2011; Groupon, annual results 2025.

B.2 VanMoof: selling direct in 32 countries, repairing in none

Sector: electric bikes. Model: a brand selling direct, online and in its own shops. Period: 2009-2023.

The success. Founded in Amsterdam in 2009 by Taco and Ties Carlier, VanMoof designs recognisable bikes, with an integrated battery, connected geolocation and anti-theft features. It sells direct, online and in shops in Amsterdam, Berlin, Paris, London, Tokyo and New York, across 32 countries, and raised more than 100 million dollars from investment funds.

The problems. The components are specific and hard to repair outside the VanMoof network, which exists only in a few cities. From 2021, the complaints multiplied: late deliveries, electrical failures, repair delays, parts that could not be found. The model is costly: production in Taiwan, logistics for heavy and fragile products, after-sales service to be built country by country.

The fall. At the end of June 2023, VanMoof stopped online sales. On 17 July 2023, the Dutch companies were declared bankrupt, with a debt of around 144 million euros. In August, the brand was taken over by Lavoie, a subsidiary of McLaren Applied.

For your project. Selling direct in a country also means providing after-sales service there. An original product with no repair network becomes a risk for the customer and for the brand. Growth financed by fundraising does not replace a positive margin per sale.

Sources: business and trade press, 2021-2023.

B.3 Wish: low prices without the trust

Sector: low-price general marketplace. Model: sellers, mostly Chinese, ship directly to Western consumers. Period: since 2010.

The rise. The most downloaded shopping app in the United States in 2017, then worldwide in 2018. Stock market flotation in December 2020, valuation of around 14 billion dollars.

The problems. Uneven quality, delivery times of several weeks, a gap between the photos and the products received. In November 2021, the French administration had Wish de-indexed from search engines and app stores, after checks revealed a high proportion of dangerous products. Then Temu (launched at the end of 2022) and Shein, with far larger advertising budgets, took its customers.

The fall. Turnover down 66% in 2022. In February 2024, the platform was sold to the Singaporean company Qoo10 for around 173 million dollars, just over 1% of its 2020 valuation.

For your project. In Europe, product compliance is not a detail: it can close off access to a market. An advantage based solely on price does not withstand a cheaper competitor still. Trust (quality, deadlines, returns) is an asset as important as traffic.

Sources: Inside Retail Asia, February 2024; DGCCRF, November 2021.

B.4 Torfs: a Belgian retailer that paid the price of omnichannel, and is holding on

Sector: shoes. Model: a Belgian chain of shops (mainly Flanders) that went omnichannel. Period: founded in 1948.

The facts. Torfs opened its online shop at the turn of the 2010s and chose to offer free delivery and returns, so as not to lose its customers to Zalando. In 2019, e-commerce accounted for 17% of its sales and grew by 20% per year, but the costs of shipping, returns, advertising and technology caused operating profit to fall from 4.1 to 1.4 million euros on 145.6 million in turnover; its Dutch shop and its presence on bol were loss-making. Wouter Torfs, then CEO, put it bluntly: “These are costs we did not have before.” The company stayed the course, integrated shops and web (in-store pick-up and returns, shared stock), and recovered its margins. In 2024, under the leadership of Lise Conix, turnover reached 206.4 million euros (+4.4%) and operating profit nearly 19 million (+11%).

For your project. E-commerce has a cost that physical retail did not have: delivery, returns, online advertising, technology. Offering free delivery and returns is a strategic choice, not a detail. Omnichannel (shop and web reinforcing each other) is the response of an established retailer; a pure online shop must find its own.

Sources: RetailDetail, 2 August 2019 and 4 April 2025.

B.5 Komono: an Antwerp brand born global

Sector: watches and sunglasses. Model: a design brand sold direct and through resellers worldwide. Period: since 2009.

The facts. Founded in 2009 in Antwerp by Raf Maes and Anton Janssens, two former professional snowboarders, Komono (“small things”, in Japanese) designs watches and glasses with an accessible design. Five years after its creation, the brand was sold in 45 countries, through a network of resellers (concept stores, department stores) complemented by an online shop. The design and the reseller network carried the brand internationally even before its site was a major channel.

For your project. For a small Belgian brand, your own site is not always the first international channel: local resellers, niche marketplaces or concept stores can open a country faster and at lower risk. The site then becomes the place for the direct relationship and for margin.

Source: Montecristo Magazine, February 2014; check the brand’s current situation before using it.

B.6 Back Market: selling trust, not just second-hand

Sector: refurbished electronics (marketplace). Model: a marketplace that connects professional refurbishers with consumers, with a guarantee and quality control. Period: since 2014.

The facts. Founded in 2014 in Paris by Thibaud Hug de Larauze, Quentin Le Brouster and Vianney Vaute. It sells refurbished smartphones (around 60% of sales), computers, tablets and consoles, present in more than 18 countries. In 2025, its gross merchandise value (GMV) is estimated at 3.5 billion dollars and its own turnover at around 495 million; the company is valued at around 5.1 billion euros — one of the few European unicorns in e-commerce.

For your project. In a second-hand market, the real product is trust: a guarantee, quality control and a right of return turn a risky purchase into a reassuring one. A clear value proposition — cheaper, greener, and guaranteed — can create an entire category. The circular economy is a promising positioning in Europe.

Source: Sacra, Back Market profile, 2025.

B.7 Coolblue: winning through service, not through price

Sector: consumer electronics. Model: an online (and shop) retailer obsessed with customer service, with delivery handled in-house. Period: since 1999.

The facts. Founded in 1999 in Rotterdam by Pieter Zwart. Present in the Netherlands, Belgium and Germany. In 2024, record turnover of 2.46 billion euros (+2.1%), EBITDA of 97.8 million (+10.8%), and a Net Promoter Score of 72 — the highest in its history. The company delivers itself (Coolblue bikes and vans) and takes care of every detail of the experience, from the packaging to the humour of its customer service.

For your project. Where prices are transparent and competition is fierce (Amazon, bol), Coolblue differentiates itself through service and the relationship — measured and managed daily thanks to the NPS. Controlling your own delivery means controlling the moment of truth. An e-commerce business can win without being the cheapest.

Source: Coolblue / Dutch IT Channel, 2024 results; RetailDetail.

B.8 Zalando: from retailer to platform

Sector: online fashion. Model: a retailer that became a platform (marketplace, logistics and services for brands). Period: since 2008.

The facts. Founded in 2008 in Berlin by Robert Gentz and David Schneider, initially on the Zappos model (shoes delivered and returned free of charge). It became the European leader in online fashion, present in around 25 countries. In 2023, turnover of around 10.1 billion euros (slightly down, -1.9%) for a gross merchandise value of around 14.6 billion; the company is aiming for 15% of the European fashion market and is transforming itself into a platform, open to third-party brands and distributors to whom it rents audience, logistics and advertising.

For your project. A retailer can become a platform: instead of just selling, it makes its audience, its logistics and its technology available to others. Free delivery and returns drove growth… and weigh on margin: at scale, the question becomes profitability, not just volume.

Sources: Zalando, annual report 2023; Just-Style, 2024.

B.9 Shein: data versus regulation

Sector: ultra-fast fashion (ultra-fast-fashion). Model: Chinese design and production driven by data, direct worldwide sales via an app. Period: since 2012.

The facts. Founded in 2012 (Chris Xu), headquarters moved to Singapore. Shein tests thousands of new models per day, produces in very small runs then restocks the successes; very low prices and massive marketing on social media. Valued at around 66 billion dollars in 2023. In Europe, it is under strong pressure: product compliance, environmental impact, working conditions, and the end of the customs exemption for small parcels (a 3-euro duty since July 2026 — see Appendix A).

For your project. Data can replace intuition: producing small, measuring, restocking what works reduces the stock risk. But a model based on volume and very low prices is directly exposed to European regulation; a regulatory advantage (duty-free small parcels) can disappear overnight.

Sources: business press 2023-2026; Appendix A for the customs framework.

B.10 Vente-Exclusive: a Belgian pioneer absorbed by a European leader

Sector: flash sales (clearing out big brands at reduced prices). Model: time-limited sales reserved for members. Period: 2006-2019 (under this name).

The facts. Founded in 2006 in Belgium by Stefan Tournoy and Léon Seynave, on the private-sales model (discounts of 30 to 70%, access reserved for members). In 2014, around 4 million members and 90 million euros in turnover. In September 2015, the French company vente-privee.com took a majority stake, with the Belgian shareholders keeping 49.9%; the whole was rebranded Veepee in 2019.

For your project. A national pioneer can succeed… then be absorbed by a European leader with the same model: in e-commerce, scale matters (purchasing, logistics, technology, reputation). For an SME, being bought out or aligning with a larger player is a possible exit, not a failure. The “members + time-limited offer” model remains a powerful lever — provided you stay honest about scarcity (see Appendix A, misleading practices).

Sources: BME.be and RetailDetail, 2015; La Libre, January 2019.

Free toolkit

Each tool fits in a few lines: what it is for, how to start, one pitfall to avoid.

Google Trends (trends.google.com). Compares interest in search terms by country and over time. Start by choosing the country at the top, then compare two or three phrasings in the local language. Pitfall: the curves are relative (100 = the maximum of the period), not volumes.

Google Keyword Planner (within a free Google Ads account). Gives monthly search volumes by country and by language. Pitfall: you must select the country and the language, otherwise the figures are those of your own country.

PageSpeed Insights (pagespeed.web.dev). Measures the speed and Core Web Vitals of a page, on mobile and desktop. Test the home page and a product page. Pitfall: a score is not an overall mark for the site; read the three indicators.

Similarweb, free version (similarweb.com). Estimates a site’s traffic, the countries its visitors come from and its traffic sources. Pitfall: the estimates are unreliable for small sites.

Wappalyzer (browser extension). Identifies a site’s platform, payment gateway and tools. Useful for competitor analysis.

Hofstede Insights, country comparison (hofstede-insights.com). The six dimensions for most countries. Pitfall: national averages, not individuals.

Access2Markets (trade.ec.europa.eu/access-to-markets). The Commission’s official tool for finding out customs duties, taxes and formalities by product and by country. You need the product’s nomenclature code (HS).

Eurostat (ec.europa.eu/eurostat). European statistics on internet use and online purchases, by country and by age. Search for “E-commerce statistics for individuals”.

Looker Studio (lookerstudio.google.com). Free dashboards connected to Google Analytics, Google Ads or a spreadsheet. For the per-market management dashboard.

Canva and Figma (free versions). Page mock-ups, personas, visuals. Figma for site mock-ups, Canva for the rest.

Miro (free version). A collaborative whiteboard for the canvas, the personas and the customer journeys.

DeepL (deepl.com). High-quality machine translation for a first version. Pitfall: have it proofread by a native speaker before publishing.

Glossary

General concepts

Cart abandonment — The customer adds items to the basket but does not complete the purchase. Leading causes: fees discovered late, a missing payment method, a mandatory account.

API — An interface that lets two pieces of software communicate: the site with the payment gateway, the carrier, the accounting system.

CAC (customer acquisition cost) — Acquisition spending divided by the number of new customers.

CLV (customer lifetime value) — The total margin a customer brings over their entire relationship with the company.

Core Web Vitals — Three Google indicators that measure a page’s display speed, responsiveness and visual stability.

D2C (direct-to-consumer) — A brand that sells directly to the consumer, without a distributor or marketplace.

Dropshipping — The seller holds no stock: the supplier ships directly to the customer.

Marketplace — A platform where many sellers offer their products (Amazon, bol, Zalando, Etsy, Vinted) in exchange for a commission.

M-commerce — Commerce conducted on a smartphone or tablet.

E-commerce platform — Software for creating and managing an online store (Shopify, WooCommerce, PrestaShop, Shopware, etc.).

Chargeback — Cancellation of a card payment at the customer’s request to their bank; costs the merchant the amount and fees.

Social commerce — A purchase made directly within a social network.

Conversion rate — The share of visitors who carry out the target action, usually the purchase.

Return rate — The share of orders sent back. High in fashion, especially in Germany.

A/B test — A comparison of two versions of a page shown at random to two groups of visitors.

Concepts specific to international trade

HS code (harmonised system) — The international code for classifying goods, which determines customs duties.

Cross-border — A sale to a customer located in a country other than the seller’s.

DAP / DDP — Two Incoterms used for delivery to the consumer: under DAP, the customer pays VAT, duties and fees at import; under DDP (delivered duty paid), the seller pays them and includes them in the price.

Geo-blocking — Blocking or redirecting a customer according to their country; regulated in the EU by Regulation (EU) 2018/302.

hreflang — A tag that tells search engines the language and country targeted by a page.

Incoterms — International Chamber of Commerce rules allocating costs and risks between seller and buyer (course unit 719105U32D2).

Direct injection — Grouping the parcels destined for a country in order to hand them over in one go to that country’s distribution network.

IOSS — A one-stop shop for VAT on goods imported from third countries, for consignments of 150 euros maximum.

Localisation — The complete adaptation of a site to a market: language, currency, units, payments, content, visuals, calendar.

OSS — A one-stop shop for declaring in Belgium the VAT due in other EU countries on distance sales.

Landed cost — The total cost for the customer: product, delivery, VAT, duties, customs clearance fees.

Payment

3D Secure 2 — A cardholder authentication protocol that implements strong authentication.

Acquirer — The institution that receives payments on behalf of the merchant.

Strong authentication (SCA) — Verification of the payer by at least two elements, required by the PSD2 directive.

Bancontact — The Belgian payment network (Bancontact cards and the Bancontact Pay app), essential for Belgian consumers.

BNPL (buy now, pay later) — Deferred or instalment payment (Klarna, payment in three instalments).

iDEAL — The dominant payment method in the Netherlands.

Payment gateway (PSP) — The provider that transmits and secures payments (Mollie, Stripe, Adyen, Worldline).

PCI DSS — The security standard for payment card data.

Tokenisation — Replacing the card number with a token that is worthless to a fraudster.

Wero — A European payment solution from the European Payments Initiative (EPI), available in Belgium.

Digital marketing

CRO — Conversion rate optimisation.

CTR (click-through rate) — The share of people who click after seeing a link or an advert.

GEO (generative engine optimization) — Techniques for being cited by AI assistants (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini).

Remarketing — Advertising shown to visitors who did not buy.

ROAS — The turnover generated per euro of advertising.

SEA — Paid search (Google Ads, Microsoft Ads).

SEO — Organic search.

Bounce rate — The share of visitors who leave the site after a single page.

Building your online store with Shopify (and its limits)

This course gives you the method: choosing a market, understanding its customers, building a strategy, writing a requirements specification. It imposes no tool on you. But at the end of every session the same concrete question comes back: “All right, but what do I build the site with?” For an SME selling internationally, the most frequent answer is Shopify. This appendix explains how you create a Shopify store, what it really costs, and — just as importantly — where its limits are. No tool is neutral: knowing what you gain and what you lose by choosing it is part of the requirements specification.

H.1 Shopify in the landscape of solutions

To build an online store, there are three main families of solutions.

Family Examples Principle You own…
Hosted SaaS Shopify, Wix, Squarespace You rent a turnkey platform, hosted by the provider Your data and your content, not the platform
Self-hosted open source WooCommerce (WordPress), PrestaShop You install free software on your own hosting Everything, but you manage everything
Marketplace only Amazon, Etsy, bol You open a shop within someone else’s marketplace Nothing: you are a tenant of an audience

Shopify belongs to the first family: it is a SaaS solution (software as a service), created in Canada in 2006, which today runs several million stores. You manage neither the server, nor security updates, nor the HTTPS certificate: everything is taken care of. In exchange, you pay a monthly subscription and you agree to work within the framework that Shopify has set. That is exactly the trade-off to understand before choosing.

The choice of solution is not the choice of channel. Remember Chapter 2: your own site or a marketplace? Shopify is a tool for building your own site (D2C). It can also connect to marketplaces (Amazon, Etsy, bol) to synchronise your products, but it does not exempt you from the channel decision.

H.2 Creating a Shopify store, step by step

Here is the typical journey, from sign-up to launch. Each step refers back to the chapter of the course that tells you what to decide; Shopify merely carries out the decision.

  1. Open a trial. Shopify offers a free trial of a few days, then often a first one to three months at a token rate. You create an account and give the store a provisional name.
  2. Choose a plan. Basic to start, an intermediate plan as volume grows (see H.3). You can change later.
  3. Connect your domain name. Buy your domain (from a Belgian registrar or from Shopify) and point it to the store. A domain of your own (yourbrand.be) inspires more confidence than a .myshopify.com subdomain — it is a trust signal in the sense of Chapter 4.
  4. Choose and customise a theme. Shopify provides free and paid themes (around 200 to 400 $ one-off for premium themes). Customisation is done without code, in blocks; advanced code uses Shopify’s own Liquid language.
  5. Create the product pages. Title, description, photos, variants (size, colour), stock, price. This is where perceived quality is decided: careful photos, descriptions that answer the objections (Chapter 6).
  6. Set up payments. Activate Shopify Payments or a third-party provider such as Mollie or Stripe. To sell in Belgium and the Netherlands, check for the presence of Bancontact and iDEAL, not just the card (Chapter 8). It is decisive: a missing local payment method causes cart abandonment.
  7. Set up delivery. Define your zones, your rates, your carriers and your pick-up points (Chapter 7). Display a delivery time you can keep to.
  8. Configure taxes. Shopify calculates VAT by country. Link this setting to your real situation: above 10,000 € of distance sales within the EU, it is the VAT of the customer’s country via the OSS one-stop shop (Chapter 7 and Appendix A).
  9. Switch the store to several languages. With Shopify Markets and the Translate & Adapt app, you manage languages, currencies and prices per market. Machine translation gets you a rough first draft; proofreading by a native speaker remains essential (Chapter 4).
  10. Add the legal pages, then test and launch. Terms and conditions of sale, legal notices, returns and privacy policy (Appendix A). Place a test order end to end (payment, confirmation email, invoice), then remove the store’s password: you are online.

For your project. You do not need to actually build the store for the assessment. But your requirements specification must describe, heading by heading (the seven from Chapter 10), what the store must do. The ten steps above are a good checklist: for each one, what do you require?

H.3 How much it really costs

The displayed price is only part of the bill. Count four items.

Item Order of magnitude (to be checked on shopify.com)
Monthly subscription Basic of the order of 25-30 € / month on an annual plan; intermediate plan ~80 €; advanced plan significantly more
Shopify transaction fees 0% if you use Shopify Payments; otherwise a surcharge of the order of 0.5 to 2% depending on the plan
Bank fees on each payment of the order of 1.5 to 2.9% + a few cents per transaction, as with any payment provider
Apps and theme from 0 to several tens of euros per month depending on the apps added; premium theme ~200-400 $ one-off

Two cost traps always come up. First, the apps that pile up: customer reviews, loyalty, emails, translation, each at 5-30 € per month, and the bill doubles without your noticing. Second, the transaction surcharge if you refuse Shopify Payments: on a turnover of 100,000 €, 1% represents 1,000 € per year that leave on top of the bank fees.

True to the rule of this course: do not take these figures as they are into your business plan. Prices change often; check them and date them.

H.4 The critique: Shopify’s limits

Shopify is popular because it is quick to launch and reliable. But a serious requirements specification also names the drawbacks.

  • You rent, you do not own. As long as you pay, all is well. The day you stop, the store goes dark. You own neither the code nor the platform: you own your customer data and your content, which you can export.
  • Dependency (lock-in). Migrating from Shopify to another solution is heavy: a theme to redo, apps to replace, URLs to redirect. The bigger the store grows, the more expensive leaving becomes.
  • The cost that climbs with success. The subscription and above all the apps increase with volume and needs. What cost 30 € at the start can exceed 200 € per month after two years.
  • Constrained customisation. You quickly get 90% of what you want, but the last 10% runs up against the framework of Shopify and Liquid. Certain very specific business logics are difficult, even impossible, without a specialist developer.
  • Rules you do not control. Shopify sets its terms, its rates and the categories of products allowed, and can change them. As with a marketplace, a suspension of your account is possible in the event of a dispute.
  • A foreign player. Shopify is Canadian/American. Your data is processed partly outside the European Union: check the GDPR clauses and the location of the hosting, especially in B2B or for sensitive data.

On the other side, the alternatives have their own trade-offs. WooCommerce (open source, grafted onto WordPress) and PrestaShop (open source, of European origin) give you back full ownership and total freedom — but you inherit the hosting, the security updates, the backups and the outages. You do not remove the cost: you shift it from rent to time and technical skill.

Common mistake. Choosing Shopify “because everyone uses it”, without having checked that it offers Bancontact and iDEAL in your markets, nor calculated what the apps you will need will cost. The right reflex: start from your requirements specification (what the site must do) and check, point by point, that the tool does it — and at what price.

H.5 Shopify or an alternative: how to decide

A simple grid, in the spirit of Chapter 2.

Your situation Reasonable option
SME, small team, no developer, fast international launch Shopify (SaaS): the time saved is worth the rent
Already a WordPress site, a tight budget, in-house technical skill WooCommerce: you reuse what you already have
Need for strong business customisation, a technical team, high volume Open source (PrestaShop, WooCommerce) or a bespoke solution
Simply testing a market, a few products A marketplace first (Etsy, Amazon, bol), then your own site

There is no universal right answer: there is the answer that is coherent with your business model, your means and your requirements specification.

H.6 BELGA CRAFT: what it chooses, and why

Remember: the maker at BELGA CRAFT already has a Shopify site, and her developer wants to “just add German and Dutch”. Her choice holds up, for three reasons: her three-person team has no technical profile to manage a server; Shopify Markets lets her open Germany and the Netherlands without rebuilding the site; and the time saved can go to the product and the marketing, where her difference is made. She knowingly accepts the limits of section H.4: the monthly rent, the dependency, and the fact that she does not own the platform. In return, she writes into her requirements specification two non-negotiable requirements — Bancontact and iDEAL at the checkout, and a human proofreading of the German and Dutch translations — precisely because these are the points where a turnkey tool can create a false impression.

For your project. In your requirements specification, devote a short paragraph to the choice of the technical solution: which one, why, and which limits you accept. A jury rarely remembers the tool chosen; it remembers that you knew what that choice implied.
